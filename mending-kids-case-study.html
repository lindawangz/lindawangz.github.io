Mending Kids
Medical Inventory

A web-based inventory system that helps Mending Kids keep track of medical supplies, prepare missions, and spend less time working around a spreadsheet.

01 · Context

Medical supplies were being managed through a process that had grown hard to maintain.

Mending Kids provides free surgical care to children around the world. Their team manages donated medical supplies and prepares them for missions, so inventory has to stay accurate from donation all the way to deployment.

10,000+ items

The existing inventory lived in a large Google Sheet, making it difficult to search, update, and keep consistent.

More than inventory

The team also had to handle missions, packing, customs records, expiration dates, and annual accounting.

02 · Problem

The real problem was the workflow around the spreadsheet.

We wanted to make the day-to-day work easier without losing the information the organization relied on.

Finding and updating suppliesA huge spreadsheet made everyday inventory work slower and more error-prone.
Keeping track of mission suppliesStaff had to manually connect inventory items to missions and packing needs.
Handling accounting and auditsDonation history and market value had to be tracked, even though they were not always part of the same workflow.
Keeping physical recordsMission information still needed to travel with physical bags and through customs.
03 · User Research

Talking through the real process changed what we built.

We walked through existing tasks with stakeholders and focused on where the current process became frustrating or repetitive.

Market value did not belong in every task.It mattered for year-end accounting, but it could get in the way of day-to-day logistics. We made it optional.
Auditing needed its own workflow.The team needed to look back at what had been donated in a given year and keep those records organized.
The digital product still had to support physical work.Mission supplies are packed into bags and moved through customs, so printing and downloadable records mattered.
Missions needed more structure.Instead of one long list, users wanted to organize supplies into practical groups for packing and deployment.

What we took away

We stopped thinking of the project as “a better inventory table.” It needed to connect inventory, missions, packing, and the work that happens after a mission.

“The market value isn't something we need until like year end...” Edin Le · stakeholder feedback
04 · From Research to Design

We mapped the workflow first, then decided what the interface needed.

The team explored different ways to create missions, reuse information from past missions, assign supplies, and close out inventory afterward.

Early workflow mapping helped us see where mission creation and inventory management were getting mixed together.

Turning findings into decisions

We used a solution matrix to decide which changes were worth prioritizing.

  • Make market value optional.
  • Separate “assign item” from general inventory actions.
  • Support printable mission and bag records.
  • Organize mission supplies into smaller groups.
Solution matrix used to prioritize ideas by effort and impact.
05 · Final Product

A single place to manage inventory and the missions that use it.

The final system brought the most important pieces of the workflow into one interface.

Inventory management

Search and track supplies with item details, quantities, status, missions, and expiration information in one place.

Dashboard + alerts

A high-level view surfaces things that need attention, including expiration and low-stock information.

Missions

Mission workflows connect supplies to real deployments instead of treating inventory as a separate list.

Designed around the team

The interface reflects the exceptions, paperwork, and handoffs that came up during research. Instead of forcing every task into the same workflow, the system supports the way the team actually works across inventory, missions, packing, and follow-up.

06 · User Testing

We tested the prototype against real tasks.

We used an unmoderated Maze study and moderated testing with Mary. Tasks were timed so we could compare how people moved through the new workflow.

Unmoderated testing

Users completed tasks in the Vercel prototype. We looked at completion, timing, and heat maps.

What changed after testing

Testing surfaced missing flexibility around market value, audits, printing, and editing item information.

07 · Results

The final prototype gave us a few clear signals.

08 · Takeaway

We designed around the work, not the spreadsheet.

The biggest lesson from this project was that the inventory itself was only one part of the problem. Once we understood how supplies move through donations, missions, packing, customs, and accounting, the product started to make much more sense.

Keep information flexible when it belongs to a later step.
Design for physical workflows even when the product is digital.
Use research to uncover missing workflows, not just visual problems.
